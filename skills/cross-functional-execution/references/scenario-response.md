# Scenario response and learning

## Separate preparation from an active event

Scenario work explores plausible futures before or alongside a decision. An active incident needs the company's authorized response path and relevant specialists. Do not hold containment or time-critical escalation until a model or memo is complete. The operator can maintain a verified picture, dependencies, internal updates, and decision records within mandate.

For security, safety, legal, employment, regulated, or disclosure matters, work through the designated functional owners and current company procedures. Do not import a generic rule that a CEO must personally approve every action or statement. Authorization depends on the actual regime and delegation.

## Build useful scenarios

Define the decision and useful time window. Select uncertainties that could change that decision, including correlated events. Use ranges or qualitative states when probabilities are unsupported. Do not multiply subjective probabilities to create a seemingly precise compound-event likelihood.

Trace the initiating event, immediate effect, affected functions, secondary consequences, and feedback loops. Show the time until impact becomes difficult to reverse and where action can interrupt it. Separate causal links supported by company data from assumptions.

For example, a key hire delay may reduce launch coverage, increase support load for existing staff, delay improvements, and raise backlog. Compare whether the response should narrow the launch, secure temporary coverage, change customer commitments, or accept a bounded delay. This is an illustrative mechanism, not a factual claim about a company.

Build scenarios that expose distinct choices. Do not automatically label one, two, and three adverse events “base,” “stress,” and “severe.” One event may be more consequential than several others. Explore upside surprises when they can strain service capacity or change resource choices.

## Response and trigger record

| Field | What to specify |
|---|---|
| Scenario and evidence | What changes, why it is plausible, which links are assumed, and the time window |
| Impact and cascade | Affected outcomes, commitments, operating services, resources, and second-order consequences |
| Early signal | Observable measure or event, source, monitoring owner, and freshness |
| Decision trigger | The condition and latest useful decision time, with uncertainty and false-alarm risk |
| Response choices | Actions now and contingent actions, costs, opportunity costs, dependencies, and limits |
| Authority | Who activates, approves resources, executes, and receives escalation |
| Recovery and review | What stabilizes the situation, what restores normal operation, and when assumptions are retested |

Distinguish a signal requiring investigation from a trigger authorizing a response. A vague concern about employee sentiment is not a reliable attrition forecast or a mandate for intrusive monitoring. Prefer proportionate, legitimate operating signals and direct evidence from the relevant owners.

A hedge has a cost even if the adverse event never occurs. Compare low-regret preparation with options that tie up scarce people, money, or time. An expensive hedge may worsen the normal plan. Name the condition where the recommendation flips.

Derive trigger timing from the response rather than choosing a convenient review frequency. If the last useful completion time is `T`, mobilization and execution take `R`, and observation plus escalation can lag `L`, the condition must become observable by `T − R − L`. Add uncertainty to the response time rather than hiding it in an unexplained score. A weekly report cannot protect a decision whose remaining window is two days. If no reliable early signal exists, compare pre-positioning, smaller exposure and acceptance of the residual risk. The [worked response](worked-delivery.md) executes this calculation.

## Pre-mortem and readiness

Imagine the plan failed by a relevant date, then gather plausible explanations from affected functions before ranking them. In [Gary Klein's version](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem), each person writes reasons alone, then reads them out one at a time. His article says imagining the failure as certain improves people's ability to identify causes by 30 percent. Klein's own co-authors later described the 1989 study differently. It measured how many reasons people generated ([Veinott and others](https://idl.iscram.org/files/veinott/2010/1049_Veinott_etal2010.pdf)). Treat it as more reasons surfaced, not proven accuracy. Distinguish evidenced risks, uncertain concerns worth testing, and claims that current evidence weakens. Do not dismiss an objection because its presenter lacks authority or because the facilitator labels it overstated.

For each material risk, name the assumption, consequence, mitigation or test, owner, date, and acceptance or escalation decision. Prioritize by consequence, likelihood evidence, time to act, and reversibility. Use numeric risk scores only if their scales are defined and helpful. A catastrophic risk should not become acceptable merely because a poorly evidenced likelihood score is low.

A readiness decision may be proceed, proceed within limits, narrow, delay, or stop. Preserve material dissent, accepted risk, fallback, and the evidence reviewed. Someone outside the room should be able to reconstruct the decision without guessing which checks occurred.

## During the event

Keep facts, hypotheses, unknowns, decisions, and actions distinct. Timestamp the situation view and cite the source of important facts. Identify the next update or decision point and the owner checking each material unknown. Do not speculate about cause to make an update feel complete.

Coordinate dependent actions with the incident or functional lead. Keep internal and external communication approval paths explicit. Drafts are not sent statements. Affected audiences and recipients depend on the actual event and company procedures. Do not turn this skill into authority to notify third parties.

Reconcile the changed capacity, priorities, and commitments once urgent conditions allow it. A resource reduction without scope changes can leave the original plan impossible. Record the work stopped, delayed, or continued, along with customer or service consequences.

## After the event

Separate outcome quality from decision quality. Reconstruct what was known at the time, expected versus actual outcomes, sequence of events, warning signals, actions, and dependencies. Consider alternative explanations and multiple contributing causes. A chain of “why” questions can guide investigation, but it does not prove a single root cause or eliminate uncertainty.

Choose concrete corrective actions tied to observed mechanisms. Record owner, due date, intended effect, verification method, and review date. Distinguish the fix being implemented from evidence that recurrence risk or operating performance improved. Verify the next relevant real execution and the longer-term effect where measurable.

A post-event review should change something when the evidence supports it, while avoiding a new rule for every rare outcome. Some decisions can be sound and still produce a poor result. Record residual risk and the condition for reopening the question.

## Failure modes

Scenario theater produces detailed adverse futures without an action path. False precision assigns confident probabilities to weak assumptions. A response can fail when the activation right is unclear, a trigger arrives too late, the same resources are promised twice, or a mitigation has no verification. Learning can fail when a narrative blames a person, declares a cause without evidence, or closes actions after documentation alone.

## Operating risk and control coordination

Record each material risk's event, affected outcome, evidence, uncertainty, controls, and owner. Include action owner, residual exposure, acceptance authority, trigger, next review, and closure evidence. Differentiate an open risk from an issue already occurring. Distinguish a control documented on paper from evidence that it operated effectively.

When audit or customer requirements are relevant, have the responsible function confirm the actual scope and applicable obligations. Coordinate a requirement-to-control-to-owner-to-evidence view, dates, evidence freshness, identified gaps, and remediation dependencies. Prioritize by material exposure and binding deadlines, not just the easiest items or a sales request. Never infer compliance from a completed checklist.

For corrective work, separate immediate containment, correction of the current occurrence, and measures addressing the supported cause. Check whether a change introduces a new failure elsewhere. Verify implementation and subsequent effectiveness, with a review period suited to the actual risk. A closed task does not itself close residual risk. Legal, security, engineering, finance, HR, and regulated quality owners retain their specialist judgments and acceptance rights.

An evidence contract names the control objective, owner, exact population and period, trigger or frequency, expected execution, retained evidence, exception route and effectiveness test. For a preventive approval control, a signed policy proves design only. Match actual changes against actual approvals, inspect timing and scope, and resolve missing or late records with the control owner. A sample can detect failure but cannot justify a claim about all transactions. Report the sample rule and bound, exceptions and residual uncertainty. Do not close an exception by creating a retrospective approval and describing it as timely.
