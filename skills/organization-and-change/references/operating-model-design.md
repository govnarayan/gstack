# Operating model design

Use this procedure when an approved direction requires choices about how capabilities, decisions, people, processes and systems work together. It can support a company, business unit, function, acquisition or separation. Start with the scope actually served and the operator's mandate. A new reporting chart alone does not specify how work will run.

The strongest case for retaining the current model is avoiding disruption to relationships, specialist expertise and ongoing service. Compare that case with the cost of unresolved interfaces, duplicate work or a capability gap. A new model earns its transition cost only if it changes the demonstrated problem.

## Establish the design question

Read the strategy, actual work, current responsibilities, customer and service obligations, staffing, costs and recent disputed decisions. Identify the sponsor, actual approvers and affected functional owners. Separate company-wide constraints from discretion held by a unit or initiative.

State the required outcome, what must keep working and the evidence that the present arrangement cannot deliver. Reconstruct representative normal work, an exception and a priority conflict. Compare unclear direction, insufficient capability or capacity, poor interfaces, incentives and grouping before selecting a structural remedy. Use [organization choices and workforce planning](organization-and-workforce.md) and the [diagnosis and transition case](diagnosis-and-transition-case.md) for that evidence procedure.

Write design requirements that can distinguish options: decisions must reach the authorized owner in time, customer context must reach delivery, scarce expertise must remain usable, or a service must continue through the change. Describe each with evidence and an acceptance test. Avoid vague requirements such as more agile without the particular delay or decision being changed.

## Work from company choices to operating work

Design at three scopes, passing evidence in both directions. A detailed process can reveal that the enterprise hypothesis is infeasible. It should trigger a recorded revision rather than an informal workaround.

| Scope | Choices to make | Evidence that carries into the next scope |
|---|---|---|
| Enterprise or served unit | Strategic requirements, boundaries, capabilities, degree of operational independence, shared obligations, resource and decision ownership | Required outcomes, common standards, permitted local variation, actual approval rights and unresolved choices |
| Function or service | Capability remit, customers, recurring outputs, delivery mode and location, process interfaces, tools, economics and service measures | Accepted service commitments, information flows, capacity, decision limits and operating dependencies |
| Team, role or subprocess | Grouping, leadership capacity, role outcomes, work sequence, access, staffing, management expectations and transition | Who performs and accepts the work, exception route, feasible timing and evidence the new arrangement operates |

Start at the lowest scope that resolves the problem. A local interface repair need not wait for an enterprise redesign. Equally, do not finalize detailed roles around an unresolved choice about customer ownership or shared capacity. Functional leaders retain design and resource decisions within their actual rights. People and Legal own employment procedures where staffed.

Choose how to build a larger design. Working through successive leadership layers can use newly appointed leaders' knowledge and increase involvement, but lengthens the sequence. Developing a complete draft before selection can expose total cost and dependencies earlier, but risks weak local evidence and acceptance. Compare the approaches by complexity, time available, decision makers, data quality and the work needed to validate the draft. A complex function can use a different approach from its neighbors if the shared interfaces still reconcile.

Decide whether central design support improves consistency enough to justify dedicated capacity and reduced local flexibility. Define what it consolidates and what functional leaders still decide. Choose an existing spreadsheet or a specialist design tool by version control, scenario comparison, data access, cost analysis and actual maintenance needs. A tool is useful only if the owners keep its evidence current.

## Separate the design dimensions

### Capability purpose and variation

Define a capability as something the business must be able to do, not the name of a department. Record its beneficiary, accepted output, activities, decisions, information, tools and required skills.

Ask two separate questions for each capability:

1. Does this work create a difference customers or the strategy need, or does it primarily need reliable, efficient execution? State the evidence. A prestigious function is not automatically differentiating.
2. Does the work need materially different product, customer or business-unit context, or can the same method and output serve several units? Identify useful variation separately from accidental inconsistency.

These questions generate candidates. Common work may be shared with clear service commitments. Business-specific routine work may need embedded context while sharing tools or standards. Differentiating expertise may need local ownership or a shared expert group with explicit priority rights. Compare alternatives against actual demand, skills, knowledge transfer, transition burden and the strategy. Neither axis determines the reporting line by itself.

Do not treat this classification as the same decision as process integration versus standardization. The [operating-model framework](../../../frameworks/references/organization-change.md#operating-model) asks what units must connect and standardize. Differentiation and business specificity ask what the capability needs to preserve. Use both only when they change the choice.

### Ownership, delivery mode and location

Decide who owns the outcome and resources before choosing where the work runs. Compare embedded delivery, a shared internal service, an expert group, an external provider or a combination. For each, identify the customer, service commitment, actual capacity, escalation and retained decision rights.

Then compare global, regional and local delivery where geography matters. Use required customer proximity, response windows, language, local requirements supplied by specialists, access to skills, coordination and full operating cost. A centralized owner can use distributed delivery. Local execution need not imply independent policy. A cheaper location can lose its apparent advantage after supervision, transfer, rework and service costs.

For shared services or outsourcing, identify work that can actually be standardized. Compare internal control and scale against vendor dependency, transition cost, dual running, quality and exit. Calculate economics under the same demand and service requirement. Do not apply a fixed labor replacement ratio or assume that moving a budget allocation removes cost. Use [resource and economic choices](../../financial-resource-decisions/references/resource-and-economic-choices.md).

### Value and operating trade-offs

For each option, explain the trade-off between cost and service quality, and between adaptability and control. These are separate comparisons, not a score to total. A required quality or control condition is a constraint. Name the evidence or threshold that changes the choice.

Check consistency across functions. A service promising fast local response cannot rely on a shared team whose priorities never include that customer. A team expected to experiment needs room to change methods, while an interface used by others may need a stable output and notification route. Preserve the useful boundary rather than choosing either complete freedom or uniformity for everything.

## Test authority in practice

For each material capability or decision, distinguish four relationships in ordinary terms:

| Relationship | What it permits | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Controls the decision | Makes the specified choice within actual limits | Authority over every budget, resource or external obligation |
| Executes the work | Delivers the agreed output with an accepting owner | Authority to change the policy or priority unilaterally |
| Influences the choice | Supplies evidence or advice to the decision owner | Approval merely because the person sponsors or attends |
| Receives information | Sees the approved result or permitted evidence | A veto or a right to direct the work |

Record actual collective approvals when required. Separate budget ownership, resource assignment, performance evaluation and outcome accountability. A person can hold different relationships for different decisions. An operator coordinating the design must not accept an outcome that depends on undelegated authority without an escalation path.

Replay a disputed priority: who can require the work, who can move the specialist, who accepts displaced service and who settles a conflict? If the proposal still relies on an interested leader informally overruling the owner, its authority problem remains.

## Test capability maturity against observed work

For a repeated operating practice, list the actual lifecycle activities and required outputs before scoring readiness. Write observable statements for the practice at relevant levels, such as an owner improvising, a repeatable method being followed, or evidence driving correction. Define the distinction using accepted work, decision speed, quality or continuity. More documentation alone need not mean a better capability.

Have the relevant owners assess those same statements, then calibrate their ratings with interviews and actual work records. Ask about a normal case, a failure or exception, what the written plan required, what happened, missing people/process/tools and the resources available for the next cycle. Preserve disagreement between self-rating and observed execution as evidence to investigate.

Record the eligible respondent population, selection, response and nonresponse, role coverage, observation window and missing evidence. A small experienced group can identify a mechanism without establishing its frequency across the company. A high score with missing operating evidence remains unverified. Do not turn a source ladder into an invented pass mark.

Produce a capability record: activity and expected output, behavior statement, self-rating, work/interview evidence, contrary evidence, coverage limits, gap, feasible corrective action, owner and next observed-use test. Prioritize the gaps that change the company's ability to execute its actual commitments. Recheck after the next relevant work rather than closing the action when a playbook is written.

## Place insight work and product investment decisions

When strategy work is dispersed, compare a central team, embedded unit teams, a shared research or market-insight service and a hybrid. Specify who owns customer/market, technology and financial evidence, who decides the resulting choice and how units contribute local knowledge. Compare response time, consistency, duplicated effort, access to expertise and competing priorities. A single owner can coordinate an insight without controlling every unit's strategy or technical decisions.

For product investment, connect customer and strategic evidence to the lifecycle: exploration, plan/design, development/test, launch, in-market change and retirement as relevant. With Product and Engineering, identify decisions requiring commercial, customer, technical, resource, Finance and control evidence. Preserve a consistent cross-functional core or an explicit handoff so knowledge does not disappear between phases. Agree capacity before treating participation as committed.

Use the authorized investment forum to decide proceed, stop or return with specific revisions. Record the bounding scope/resources, evidence reviewed, unresolved conditions, actual decider and next decision date. A stop needs reasons. A return needs the missing evidence or change and a time for reconsideration. Test customer value, strategy fit, economics, risk, feasibility and available resources rather than allowing a technical progress update to stand in for the investment decision.

Keep product, pricing, portfolio and resource rights distinct where the company assigns them to different owners. Use [product direction and delivery](../../product-and-market/references/direction-and-delivery.md) for requirements, release and outcome evidence, and [commercial operating interfaces](../../cross-functional-execution/references/commercial-operating-interfaces.md) for the dependent selling and service choices.

## Preserve capabilities through a combination or separation

Compare the unit's capability with the parent or receiving organization's capability before deciding to absorb it. Identify where it is weaker, comparable or stronger, and what makes the advantage transferable: people, routines, relationships, data, tools, incentives or customer trust. Record what is still unknown.

Compare relative scale using the relevant revenue, workforce, customer and operating load, alongside business-model differences in customers, distribution, footprint and ways of working. Use these inputs to choose scope, leadership capacity and pace by capability or function. A small unit can still impose a large systems or customer dependency, so transaction size alone does not establish a light transition.

Compare retaining the capability, borrowing an existing service, strengthening it locally, learning from it across the company, integrating selected interfaces or absorbing the work. The same unit can need different choices by capability. A routine service gap and a distinctive customer relationship need not follow one integration posture.

Test where the proposed change puts value under pressure: departure of a key specialist, replacement of a working routine, lost customer ownership, changed rewards, inaccessible data, tool migration or slower decisions. Compare the efficiency gain with retention, service, adoption and transition consequences. People owns approved retention actions. Commercial owners validate customer continuity. Technical and control owners validate systems and access.

Specify conditions before changing the capability: knowledge transfer demonstrated, successor capacity available, critical people and relationships covered, required permissions working and the receiving owner able to operate. Define when to preserve the arrangement longer, stage integration or reverse a reversible change. Talent deal or technology deal is not sufficient evidence for a uniform speed or distance.

### Critical roles and continuity over time

With People and functional leaders, identify roles whose loss would interrupt a necessary capability, customer relationship or transition dependency. Record scarce knowledge, available backup, replacement and ramp time, workload, and the date continuity is needed. Criticality is a work dependency. It does not establish that a named person intends to leave.

Use legitimate evidence from the responsible owners to assess availability risk, with uncertainty explicit. Compare role criticality and continuity risk separately. For the affected segments, compare knowledge transfer, backup capacity, management or work changes, a staged transition and authorized retention arrangements. A blanket monetary offer can miss the reason continuity is at risk. People and Finance own terms, budget and employment decisions.

Build a time-based continuity record: role/capability, evidence and limits, disruption if unavailable, backup/replacement path, action owner, authorized commitment, milestone and review date. Include the period after an incentive, temporary assignment or promised arrangement ends. Where a two-year view fits the assignment, inspect the second year's coverage and retention conditions rather than assuming the first year's plan protects it. Do not infer a universal departure date or a prediction from a sentiment score.

### Behavior, culture and pressure points

Describe the same operating situations for both groups: how priorities change, who may challenge a leader, how customer exceptions are decided, what information is shared and what performance is rewarded. Use matched statements of observable behavior rather than different survey questions or broad culture labels. Combine interviews, permitted survey evidence and observed decisions. Report who is missing and how small or selected samples limit interpretation. Do not promise anonymity the collection cannot support.

For each consequential behavior, record an explicit intent to preserve it, combine useful elements or change it, with a business reason. Test an actual pressure point where the intentions conflict: a deadline, a customer promise, scarce capacity or an exception to a control. Identify which priority prevails, who decides, which group loses something and what changes in incentives, tools or management behavior. Apparent resistance can expose a conflict in the design.

Carry the result into the capability and transition records: observed behavior, desired behavior, preserve/combine/change intent, evidence and uncertainty, pressure-point decision, owner, resources and adoption test. Inspect decisions and work after the change. Agreement with a statement or training attendance does not establish that people can operate that way. Use [management support and change](management-and-change.md) for affected-group support and sustained adoption.

## Reconcile growth, replacement and recruiting capacity

Use the existing [workforce planning method](organization-and-workforce.md#workforce-plan) to translate work into capacity before choosing positions. Separate new capacity for growth from replacement of departures, internal transfers, vacancies and planned reductions. Backfilling every departure is a choice to justify against required work.

Reconcile staffed population by role and period:

`closing staffed population = opening staffed population + external starts + transfers in − exits − transfers out`

Internal transfers cancel in a company-wide view but can create a gap in the sending function. Reconcile authorized positions and vacancies separately from staffed people. Keep recruiting, start date, ramp and productive capacity distinct, including the existing staff time used to interview and train.

For needed starts, inspect the recruiting cohorts behind stage conversions and elapsed times with People and hiring managers. Retain comparable roles, entry period, qualified candidates, interviews, offers, acceptances, starts, open cases and withdrawals. Use those observations to work backward from starts needed by a date to the recruiting activity and interviewer capacity required. Do not apply a generic funnel rate or treat pending candidates as failures or hires.

Produce a workforce bridge: growth work and productive capacity needed, replacement assumptions, transfers, starts by date, ramp, recruiting-stage evidence, available hiring/interview capacity, cost and service coverage before the hires contribute. Show which missing conversion or timing input prevents a commitment. Compare sequencing, redeployment, narrower work or bounded external support when the pipeline cannot supply the required capacity. This supports resource planning. Recruiting operations and employment decisions remain with their owners.

When management structure is the diagnosed constraint, define the whole role population before analyzing spans and layers. Separate people managers from technical or project leads, and inspect layer counts, span distributions, small reporting groups and managers of managers by comparable work. Compare each proposed change with supervisory burden, specialist requirements, interdependence and capacity. Preserve justified exceptions rather than forcing everyone toward an average.

If estimating savings, identify only the roles addressable under the supported design and apply assumptions to that population. Reconcile the result against both total and addressable labor cost, with cash timing, releveling, transition and service effects separate. A low span does not identify an employee to remove, and a proposed structural saving is unverified until the approved change and resulting cost or capacity evidence exist.

Use [transitions and continuity](../../cross-functional-execution/references/transitions-and-continuity.md) for process dispositions, temporary services, costs and operational proof. Use [capability sourcing](../../strategy/references/capability-sourcing.md) when ownership or an external relationship is still a choice.

## Produce the decision record and run it

Use one row per consequential capability or interface. Preserve blanks as missing evidence and avoid collecting every field for a trivial change.

| Record | Required decision evidence |
|---|---|
| Purpose and scope | Strategic requirement, beneficiary, current problem, accepted output and ongoing obligation |
| Capability needs | Differentiating mechanism, business-specific context, common work, evidence and competing explanation |
| Alternatives | Keep, repair interface, regroup, share, embed, outsource or staged combination. Strongest keep case and displaced work |
| Operating choice | Current, interim and end state. Owner, delivery mode/location, processes, tools, skills and service commitments |
| Authority | Decision and resource rights, execution, influence, information, actual approvers and conflict route |
| Economics and capacity | Comparable cost, scarce skills, transition burden, recurring cost, feasible timing and what would change the choice |
| Value preservation | Capability at risk, pressure point, retention/relationship/knowledge conditions and staged change trigger |
| Proof and transfer | Work episode to replay, acceptance evidence, observed trial, remaining risks, enduring owner and review condition |

Run the proposed model on actual supplied work. Test an exception as well as normal delivery, and reconcile capacity across simultaneous demands. Report a supported no-change decision when it wins. If a trial is authorized, observe whether the intended delay, quality, authority or customer problem improves. An approved chart, charter or completed training is not evidence that the operating model works.

This procedure provides decision support and coordination for an internal Strategy & Operations, BizOps or Chief of Staff mandate. At a small startup, explicitly delegated work can be broad. At a staffed or public company, the operator prepares the analysis, records and transition evidence while functional leaders retain their decisions and specialist responsibilities.
