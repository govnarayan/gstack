# From stalled work to an organization decision

Use this reference when an organization complaint must become a tested intervention. The procedure and case are original synthesis. All people, figures, policies, and results below are synthetic. They are practice inputs, not benchmarks.

## Build the diagnosis from work episodes

Select a completed normal case, a stalled case, and a priority conflict crossing the disputed boundary. Use timestamps, work records, and the affected owners' accounts to reconstruct each. Keep active work, waiting, rework, and deliberate holds separate. Record the population and sampling rule before counting causes. If the sponsor selected only failures, do not report their frequency as a company rate.

For each suspected cause write a competing explanation and a discriminating observation. A long queue could mean insufficient capacity, unclear priority rights, missing intake information, or a specialist skill gap. Moving the reporting line answers only some of these. Ask what would still fail after the proposed reorganization.

Produce a short evidence record:

| Field | What to retain |
|---|---|
| Episode and boundary | Requested result, participants, dates, work and queue time, customer or service consequence |
| Rights in practice | Who requested, reprioritized, approved, performed, and bypassed the formal owner |
| Resource test | Work arriving versus usable capacity by scarce skill, including ongoing obligations and transition work |
| Cause and rival | Evidence for each, missing observation, and smallest test that separates them |
| Decision implication | Interface repair, added capability/capacity, manager support, regrouping, or no change |

Use the [Star Model](../../../frameworks/references/organization-change.md#star-model) when structure, information flow, rewards, and people policies must change together. Use [operating-model choices](../../../frameworks/references/organization-change.md#operating-model) when the question is what units share or standardize. Use [ADKAR](../../../frameworks/references/organization-change.md#adkar) to diagnose adoption after defining the change. These methods answer different questions.

## Continuous case: two products, one specialist team

A US Series B software company has 140 employees. The CoS has `role.authority=influence_without_authority`; the COO sponsors the review. Product leaders want separate integration teams because customer launches wait. Engineering owns specialists and technical feasibility; People owns role and reporting changes; Finance validates incremental cost. The decision covers one shared team, not the company organization.

The examined record is all 12 launch requests received during a four-week period, including completed and open requests. Eight lost at least two days awaiting a priority decision. Two of those eight also needed intake rework. Four had no priority delay. Work records show demand of 24 specialist days for launches and 12 for maintenance, incidents, and existing customers. Three specialists each have 16 usable days in the period after leave and fixed duties: 48 days total. No overtime or assumed hiring is included.

The 36-day total fits the 48-day envelope, but timing and skills still matter. Replaying the busiest week finds 10 available specialist days against 9 required, with the needed skills present. The queue is not proved harmless by a monthly average; the peak replay supplies the missing capacity check. Owners confirm requests were repeatedly paused while product leaders disagreed on precedence.

| Explanation | Observation that would support it | Observed result and implication |
|---|---|---|
| Too little total capacity | Required work exceeds usable capacity, or peaks cannot be sequenced | 36/48 days and peak 9/10 fit. Do not add staff as the first remedy. |
| Unclear priority rights | Work waits despite available specialist time; rival leaders can change order | Eight requests waited for a priority decision. Test one final queue owner. |
| Missing capability | Qualified specialist unavailable for a required task | None identified in the replay. Preserve skill coverage in the trial. |
| Wrong grouping | Stable distinct needs require repeated local trade-offs the shared owner cannot resolve | Plausible future issue, not established by these 12 requests. |

The strongest argument for dedicated teams is faster local decisions and deeper product context. The opportunity cost is losing pooled coverage before demand supports duplication. Retaining the current arrangement without fixing rights preserves expertise but leaves the observed conflict unresolved.

### Compare feasible designs

| Option | Resource and authority effect | Why select or reject now |
|---|---|---|
| Keep current arrangement | 36 days of demand, 48 available; both product heads can interrupt | Reject: leaves eight observed priority delays unexplained and untreated. |
| Keep pooling, repair the interface | Engineering director owns one queue under COO-approved priority rules; two specialist days fund setup | Select for a four-week trial. It targets the evidenced cause with reversible cost. |
| Split into two teams | With three indivisible specialists, allocate two to A and one to B: 32 usable days versus 16. Under balanced demand of 18 days each, B is already two days short. | Defer. If B surges to 28 days while A needs eight, B is 12 days short despite pooled demand of only 36/48. Borrowing A's specialists restores a shared priority dependency. A nominal 1.5/1.5 allocation is fractional sharing, not independent dedicated coverage. |
| Hire and then split | Adds cost and future capacity after recruitment and ramp | Reject as immediate remedy. There is no evidence that a hire resolves the present decision delay. |

No weighted score is needed: one option directly tests the cause while preserving the scarce capability. Finance records no incremental payroll saving from the trial. Setup consumes two existing specialist days, leaving 46 against 36 required during transition.

### Populate the interface and transition record

| Item | Agreed operating rule |
|---|---|
| Intake | Product lead provides customer impact, actual deadline, technical scope, and evidence of the commitment. Incomplete requests return to that lead. |
| Priority | Engineering director orders accepted work within the approved envelope. A product lead may request a change but cannot directly interrupt a specialist. |
| Conflict | COO decides when two approved commitments cannot both fit. The record names displaced work and customer consequence. |
| Protection | Twelve days remain planned for ongoing obligations. The service owner can request more for an incident; the director then exposes the launch trade-off. Twelve is a forecast, not a guarantee of service. |
| Technical decisions | Engineering retains design, quality, feasibility, and incident authority. The CoS records choices and follows through. |
| Transition | Week one: reconcile all open requests, practice a conflicting-priority example, publish the queue owner, and retire competing private queues. Fund two days for this work. |
| Stop/revise | Pause launch commitments if service obligations cannot be covered. Reopen the design if priority bypasses continue or the demand/skill replay no longer fits. |

The COO tells both product heads which decision right changed. The Engineering director rehearses an urgent exception with them before use. A training announcement alone would not test whether the sponsor upholds the boundary.

### Observe and decide again

In the synthetic trial, all 10 new requests have complete intake and a recorded priority decision. One is delayed for technical work, none for unresolved priority. Ongoing work uses 14 rather than the forecast 12 days; launch work uses 24, setup uses two, and total use is 40 of 48. The service owner confirms the actual obligations were met. There were two attempted bypasses; the director redirected both and the COO backed the decision.

Keep pooling with the repaired interface, record the service-demand miss, and revise the next capacity forecast. This is evidence that the arrangement worked during the observed period, not proof that the change alone caused every improvement. The test does not establish performance under a new product launch or specialist absence.

The choice flips when recurring product-specific work and peak coverage cannot fit the shared arrangement, or when distinct product decisions require authority the queue owner cannot exercise. For the illustrated 48-day pool, forecast demand above 48 is a visible total-capacity failure; below 48 can still fail because of peaks or skill mismatch. Do not substitute a headcount threshold for this replay.

## Hiring and manager support use the same evidence chain

If the capacity test instead justified a hire, create a role record before a title. For example: “Own integration delivery for the second product after a supervised ramp; handle its recurring deployment decisions within Engineering's release rules.” Name retained Engineering rights, People review, manager time, hiring/ramp assumptions, and the temporary coverage plan. Do not credit a requisition with immediate capacity.

A completed assessment row should connect the work to evidence. Capability: diagnosing an incomplete integration request. Method: the same fictional case for each candidate. Evidence: the candidate identifies the missing dependency and asks for the customer deadline before promising delivery. Assessment: meets the defined standard. Gap: no evidence yet on incident coordination. The authorized hiring manager decides or commissions that remaining evidence. A polished answer to an unrelated question cannot fill it.

For an overloaded manager, list recurring coaching, decisions, hiring, coordination, and direct delivery hours against actual available time. Test reducing direct delivery or delegating a decision before adding a layer. If a new leader is selected, use first-work completion and independently exercised decisions as handback evidence, rather than a calendar date alone.

## Delivery test

The final organization recommendation must let a reviewer replay a conflict: who decides, with what resource, whose work waits, and who reinforces the boundary. A chart without that answer remains incomplete. Compare observed behavior and service consequences with the baseline, retain contrary cases, and distinguish design failure from capacity, incentive, or adoption failure.
