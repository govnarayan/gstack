# Executed executive support

These examples are synthetic. All figures, time windows, delegations and observed results are supplied or constructed for the examples. The comparisons are original operating judgments, not company benchmarks.

## 1. Recover attention without creating a second approval chain

Question: “Can the Chief of Staff take routine approvals off the CEO's plate?” A US 120-person venture-backed SaaS company has staffed Finance and functional leaders. The CoS prepares recommendations and coordinates implementation but has no spend authority. The CEO can amend the relevant internal delegation within the company's existing governance. The Finance owner confirms which requests can follow a standard route and which need specialist approvals. Existing accounting, contract and payment controls remain separate.

Two observed weeks contain 32 requests, or 16 per week, each taking eight CEO minutes. Twenty requests satisfy the same existing policy conditions; twelve are true exceptions. All requests are counted, including returned and rejected ones, so the baseline does not omit difficult cases. Current CEO time is `16 × 8 = 128 minutes/week`. There is no evidence yet that a particular response-time target is necessary, so the proposal does not invent one.

Compare three feasible responses. A scheduling assistant can collect material, but cannot resolve an unclear spend delegation. A CoS routing service would still need a real approval boundary. A standing delegation to Finance can place the recurring decision with its existing functional owner. The strongest case for CoS routing is a temporary cross-functional gap while Finance establishes the process. It weakens once Finance can run it directly.

Use two ledgers: leader minutes and total recurring person-minutes. Do not count an avoided meeting and its constituent decisions twice. Include review, correction, failed submissions, setup and the recipient's additional work. Keep attention quality and decision latency alongside time; assigning a dollar value to every CEO minute would not establish economic value.

The modeled changes concern the ten standard requests each week. Six exceptions still take eight CEO minutes each. Both proposed routes add a 20-minute weekly CEO review and ten CEO minutes for corrections during the trial, making the forecast `48 + 20 + 10 = 78 CEO minutes/week`. The apparent return is 50 CEO minutes.

| Alternative | Additional non-CEO handling | Setup spread across six trial weeks | Net total time returned versus current CEO-only baseline |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| CoS routes and prepares ten requests | 10 × 5 = 50 min/week | 180 / 6 = 30 min/week | 50 − 50 − 30 = **−30 min/week** |
| Finance uses an accepted standing delegation | 10 × 3 = 30 min/week | 120 / 6 = 20 min/week | 50 − 30 − 20 = **0 min/week** |

Existing work common to all options cancels in this comparison. Any extra downstream workload must be added. The delegation route could still be worthwhile because it transfers 50 minutes from the CEO to a suitable operating owner without increasing modeled total effort. It is not an organization-wide time saving during the trial. The six-week horizon is an explicit test assumption, not a standard onboarding period.

For sensitivity, let `N` be standard requests per week, while six exceptions and the trial review remain constant. Delegation returns `8N − 30` CEO minutes and adds `3N + 20` other minutes, so net trial saving is `5N − 50`. At ten requests it is zero; above ten it becomes positive under these handling assumptions. After setup, modeled net is `5N − 30`, positive above six. Low volume or higher correction effort can favor direct approval. If the CEO review costs 50 rather than 20 minutes, the trial threshold rises from ten to sixteen standard requests. A headcount label cannot resolve these trade-offs.

### Completed recommendation memo

> Approve a six-week trial of a standing Finance delegation for requests that meet the existing policy conditions. Keep exceptions with the CEO and the relevant specialist owners. The trade-off is 50 modeled CEO minutes returned each week with no modeled total time saving during setup; a CoS routing service would add 30 net minutes per week and preserve a central dependency.
>
> Finance confirms the decision boundary and runs the ten weekly standard requests. The CoS documents that accepted boundary, instruments the trial and then exits recurring routing. No contract, payment, hiring or other approval is implied by this delegation. The CEO must approve the defined change before any request uses it.
>
> Decide before the first affected request. Review all trial cases for turnaround, CEO review and correction minutes, missing evidence, unauthorized decisions and new work passed to teams. Return exceptions immediately to their proper owners. Continue only if attention returns without material deterioration in decision quality or control performance. Reopen the choice if volume, review cost or the mandate changes.

This is a proposed memo until the authorized decision occurs. Its recommendation is functional delegation, not expanded CoS ownership. Validate policy conditions with their actual owner rather than inventing a generic monetary threshold.

### Facilitation and decision output

A 30-minute internal meeting is chosen because the decision and preparation fit that limit. Minutes 0–5 establish the request population, present rights and exact proposed boundary. Minutes 5–15 compare the two routes and their time ledgers. Finance's objection is exposure from misclassified exceptions, not the arithmetic. Minutes 15–25 walk two standard cases, a returned request and a contractual exception through the proposed route. The contractual exception fails the delegation test and remains with its existing owner. The final five minutes give the CEO the unresolved risk choice and read back the decision.

The synthetic decision record is: “CEO approves the six-week trial for the verified standard category only, starting after Finance accepts the written boundary and the route is tested. Exceptions retain existing approvals. Finance owns routine execution. CoS owns setup and a six-week outcome review, then exits. Finance's concern about misclassification remains a monitored risk.” Discussion did not imply authority to send requests, change systems or commit spend before those conditions were met.

In the synthetic first week, Finance handles all ten standard requests, rejects one incomplete submission for correction within that population, and sends six exceptions through the existing route. CEO time is 48 minutes for exceptions, 20 for review and 15 for correction, or 83 minutes. Returned attention is 45 minutes, five below the forecast. Finance handling is 35 minutes rather than 30. Charging all 120 setup minutes to this first week gives `45 − 35 − 120 = −110 net minutes`. Spreading setup across the six-week trial gives `45 − 35 − 20 = −10 minutes/week` if this handling pattern continues; that is a comparison over the trial horizon, not the observed first-week cost. The return and correction are included, not added as an eleventh standard request. Do not report “50 minutes saved” after this result.

Finance then runs the next cycle without CoS routing and demonstrates the exception path. One successful cycle supports transfer of execution, while the six-week outcome review remains open. If the process still needs the CoS for every edge case, the handoff has failed even if the document is complete.

## 2. Prepare a governance contribution that survives questions

Assignment: a Strategy & Operations lead in a large US public-company business unit prepares its executive sponsor's operating contribution to an established governance pack. The operator supports that contribution; Finance and the governance owners retain their records and approvals. The question is whether a rollout is on its approved trajectory and whether a revised commitment is warranted.

The approved plan names 60 teams for activation by quarter end. The source owner verifies 45 active teams, 50 teams enabled and 60 planned. An incoming slide says “90% adoption; on track.” The arithmetic `45 / 50 = 90%` is valid for uptake among enabled teams. It does not establish coverage of the commitment, which is `45 / 60 = 75%`. The difference is meaning, not a rounding error.

The operator reconciles the full set of 15 inactive planned teams with the delivery owners. Eight lack specialist coverage (five enabled but not yet active, three not enabled), five await a shared platform dependency and are not enabled, and two not enabled had an approved scope change. Each team appears in exactly one category in this stated snapshot. The bridge is `60 − 8 − 5 − 2 = 45`. The ten not enabled reconcile as `3 + 5 + 2 = 10`, while 45 active plus five enabled-but-inactive reconcile to 50 enabled. The two scope changes do not silently reduce the original baseline to 58. The latest forecast and its authorization remain separate fields. The eight coverage cases and five dependency cases require different remedies; a single “execution issue” label conceals the decision.

The resulting operating contribution is:

> Activation is 45 of 60 planned teams (75% of the original commitment), with 45 of 50 enabled teams active (90% uptake). Fifteen planned teams remain inactive: eight awaiting specialist coverage, five awaiting the shared platform, and two removed through an approved scope change. The original commitment remains visible. The sponsor is asked to approve a revised operating commitment only after the two delivery owners provide accepted dates and capacity; the current evidence does not support “on track.”

The strongest case for retaining the 90% figure is that it measures whether enabled teams use the rollout. Keep it as a separate adoption measure rather than deleting useful evidence. The strongest case against an immediate revised target is that lowering it before reconciling capacity could excuse a recoverable miss. Prepare recovery and postponement choices for the sponsor rather than fabricating a new forecast.

The working pack links the baseline, source population and as-of date, category assignments, prior forum requests and their closure evidence. Carry the open request for five platform dates forward with its accountable owner. Rehearse: “Why are 10 teams not enabled?”, “Which coverage can be moved without hurting existing service?”, “What is approved versus proposed?”, and “What observation would change the forecast?” Missing answers stay missing with an owner and decision date. The sponsor approves the contribution through the existing pack process; publication or contact with directors is a separate authorized action.

## Context changes the operating design

At a founder-held startup, the first case can use one accepted delegation note and direct exception review, possibly with the operator temporarily doing the work. Without a staffed Finance owner, select an actual receiving owner before promising a handoff. In a scaled company, compare existing approval services and delegated functional owners before introducing CoS routing. In a public-company unit, confirm which delegation belongs locally and which belongs to enterprise functions; executive sponsorship cannot supply rights it lacks. Preserve these boundaries in the memo and decision record, not only in a general disclaimer.
