# Communication and decision materials

## Start from the reader's decision or action

Determine the audience, purpose, sender, time period, confidentiality, existing format, and what the reader should decide, understand, or do. Read the source material fully and separately recap what it asks before making substantive edits. Keep the approved scope distinct from adjacent suggestions.

Build a small evidence record while reading: claim, source and location, date or period, definition, and whether it is observed, inferred, or unresolved. Reconcile conflicting numbers with the data owner. Never select the more convenient value or blend actual results, forecasts, and aspirations. Different audience framing may change detail and emphasis, but must not change the underlying facts.

Test the argument before polishing. Write its chain as problem → mechanism → evidence → proposed action → expected result. Ask which link the strongest alternative contests. “The leader spends time approving requests” does not establish that a CoS should inherit approval. The missing mechanism may be a delegation boundary or incomplete submissions. The [worked case](worked-executive-support.md) demonstrates a recommendation that changes after this comparison.

## Useful artifact forms

| Need | Content that makes it useful |
|---|---|
| Decision memo | Recommendation and trade-off first. State the decision owner, deadline, problem, alternatives including retaining the current approach, evidence, resource implications, downside, and what would reverse the recommendation. Distinguish approval from advice. |
| Strategy narrative | Explain the choice, why it addresses the problem, what waits, assumptions, implications for teams, and what evidence would prompt reconsideration. A persuasive story cannot repair unsupported logic. |
| Leadership update | Lead with material change, then risk or blocker, then help or decision required from a named person by a date. Link stable metrics to their maintained source. Preserve leads' accountability when synthesizing their input. |
| All-hands | Explain current state, why priorities matter, what changes for teams, and what waits. Budget real question time, prepare the difficult questions, and give unanswered items an owner and response date. A written record should let an absent employee understand the message. |
| FAQ or incident note | Give verified answers and their source. Separate known facts, unknowns, actions underway, guidance to the reader, and next update. Restrict personal or incident details to the appropriate audience. Coordinate sensitive timing and statements with the accountable functions. |
| Process document | Capture how work actually happens: trigger, scope, owner, steps and outputs, interfaces, exceptions, checks, and how to know it worked. Distinguish current practice from proposed changes. Include maintenance ownership. |

A status report is not an approved decision. A meeting transcript is not a commitment. Keep references to the maintained decision and action records rather than creating competing copies. A larger company may have several authoritative systems for different scopes. Reconcile them through ownership and links instead of forcing one company-wide sheet.

## Drafting and review

Consider materially different structures before polishing when the core argument is unsettled. Select the structure that answers the actual reader's question. Resolve the hardest reasoning first, then make the opening accurate to the completed argument. Preserve the author's intended meaning while editing.

Test the complete document from a reader's perspective: can they recover the recommendation, why it beats the alternatives, evidence quality, and expected action without private context? Check contradictions across sections and recent messages. Do not equate a fluent narrative with verified facts. For a consequential document, an independent reader check can expose implicit assumptions, subject to the available review arrangements.

## Match the author's voice and audience

Use the author's stated preferences and examples supplied for the task. Ask about missing style choices only when they materially affect the result. Use plain sentences, a clear opening, explicit trade-offs, and enough evidence for the audience to assess the argument. Prefer tables for comparisons and lists only when they improve readability.

Adapt tone and detail to the channel. Peer chat may need a brief answer while executive and board material may need a formal recommendation and supporting evidence. When disagreeing, address the disputed claim or assumption and acknowledge the counterpart's stated priority. Preserve the author's intended meaning without copying sample numbers, company stories, or quotations as facts.

Check the completed draft against the requested tone, terminology, format, and sender identity. A supplied example informs style but does not override the user's current request or establish facts for new writing.

## Specify the principal's involvement before speaking for them

[Claire Hughes Johnson](https://growth.eladgil.com/book/the-role-of-the-ceo/decision-making-and-managing-executives-an-interview-with-claire-hughes-johnson/) describes documenting how a founder participates. Ask the principal for a recent decision they delegated, one they wanted to see early and one they reserved. Record the differences in the mandate. Confirm the boundary against the next real decision.

[Kevin Fishner](https://review.firstround.com/focus-on-your-first-10-systems-not-just-your-first-10-hires-this-chief-of-staff-shares-his-playbook/) distinguishes running company systems from managing an executive's day. A chief of staff can own the review process while a functional leader owns its decision. Put both names on the pre-read. If the principal intervenes privately, record who now has authority before announcing the outcome.

## Write the COO role around the founder

[Emilie Choi on The Peel](https://www.thespl.it/p/the-coo-playbook-with-emilie-choi) describes dividing functions with Brian Armstrong while retaining room for mutual challenge. List the decisions the founder wants to keep, the ones awaiting an operator, and the occasions either leader should challenge the other.

Before appointing a permanent deputy, review work the candidate already led under pressure. Where the evidence is thin, give a scoped operating mandate and review it after a consequential decision. Preserve a weekly opportunity for each leader to raise a specific point of friction.
