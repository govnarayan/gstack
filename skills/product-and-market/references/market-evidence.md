# Market and competitive evidence

## Define what is being compared

Name the customer problem, buying unit, geography, use case, period, and product boundary. Include current workarounds, internal build, manual labor, and doing nothing alongside direct products. Define a segment by a meaningful difference in need, buying behavior, ability to reach it, or cost to serve. A job title alone rarely supplies all of those.

Separate market segmentation, behavioral user segmentation, and an ideal-customer profile drawn from retained and expanding customers. A prospect segment describes a potential market. An observed user segment describes the current sample. A best-customer profile can guide focus but may reflect who the current distribution happens to reach. Use disqualification criteria and explicit gaps rather than an invented representative persona.

Compare candidate segments on problem urgency, present spending, authority to buy, adoption constraints, reachability, competition, reference value, and economics. Name why a segment might be attractive but unavailable to this company. Test whether the next segment trusts the first segment's proof and can use the same product. Evaluate the focused initial segment and the complete product and service it needs. Expansion depends on transferable evidence rather than fixed adoption or market-share targets.

## Size an opportunity with an auditable boundary

Use the total addressable market, serviceable market, and obtainable market only when those distinctions answer the decision. Label units clearly. A market can be counted in accounts, users, transactions, spending, or company revenue. Those are different quantities.

Build a bottom-up range from eligible buying units × realistic annual spending or company revenue per unit. For usage models, expose volume, price, eligible use cases, and frequency separately. For marketplaces, distinguish gross transaction value from the company's revenue, including its share of transaction value. Do not multiply the total revenue of customer industries by a customer count and call it product demand.

Constrain the serviceable portion by actual product capability, buyer budget, integration, distribution, support, and geography. Build obtainable revenue from acquisition and delivery capacity: reachable accounts or users, conversion, timing, retention, ramp, implementation, and capacity. Reconcile it with the serviceable population. A convenient percentage of a large market is not an acquisition plan.

Where a top-down source exists, compare its category definition, year, currency, geography, and included revenue with the bottom-up boundary. Explain gaps instead of averaging incompatible estimates. A new category may have no credible published total. State that gap and show a range supported by buyer counts and spending evidence. Current pricing, population, market shares, and growth rates need dated sources. Estimates remain estimates even when a report repeats them.

Show sensitivity to the few assumptions that drive the result, and distinguish current spending from potential value created. A large market does not prove that buyers will switch, that this firm can reach them, or that its economics work.

## Compare competitors on buyer-relevant dimensions

Use a comparison table with capability or buying criterion, observed evidence, source/date, availability conditions, and implication. Separate published promise, verified product behavior, customer report, and analyst inference. Lack of a public feature listing does not prove that the feature is absent. Use the product where appropriate or label the capability unknown. Do not award security certification, market share, performance, or support quality from a badge or a score alone.

Compare features, price, usability, performance, documentation, support, integrations, security, scale, brand, community, and innovation only where they affect this buyer's decision. Do not total equally weighted scores into a verdict when the buyer would reject the product on one requirement. A frequent release schedule does not establish useful innovation. A missing enterprise tier does not establish inability to scale.

Build a positioning view around actual alternatives, differentiated capability, resulting value, target buyer, and credible proof. Draw map axes from differences buyers care about. Treat predictions of a competitor's response as hypotheses supported by incentives, capability, and prior behavior. Job postings or messaging changes are signals, not proof of a hidden roadmap.

For a sales battlecard, include where each product is suitable, where it wins or loses, sourced price/packaging, proof, objections, and claims to avoid. Keep unverified claims out of the usable talk track. Refresh on changes relevant to its use. Choose review triggers or a calendar schedule based on volatility and decision use rather than automatically imposing both.

## Outputs and failure modes

A market brief includes definition, segment comparison, size range with formulas, evidence limitations, obtainable opportunity tied to capacity, and product implications. A competitive brief includes the alternative set, buyer-relevant differences, sources/dates, unknowns, likely responses marked as predictions, and the test or decision each finding changes.

Reject invented share, funding used as product-quality evidence, and reviews treated as market prevalence. Check overlapping totals and price periods. Market size does not establish product-market fit. Corporate decisions about entering a market or reallocating the company portfolio belong to leadership strategy. This reference supplies the market evidence for that choice.
