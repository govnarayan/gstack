# Choices and evidence

Choose analytical methods because they answer a contested question. Do not fill a canvas unless it helps resolve the question.

| Question | Useful analysis | What can invalidate the result |
|---|---|---|
| What is the binding challenge? | Separate symptoms, competing explanations, and the constraint that limits progress. Examine customer, operating, economic, and organizational evidence. | A chosen explanation ignores contradictory data or changes with the stakeholder telling the story. |
| Where should we compete? | Compare segments, customer problems, alternatives, reachability, willingness to pay, and capability fit. | Large market estimates substitute for an obtainable opportunity or access to buyers. |
| Why would the approach win? | Trace value delivered, advantage, cost, adoption, and competitor response. Distinguish existing advantage from one expected only after scale. | The plan requires several unproven advantages simultaneously or assumes competitors remain still. |
| Which actions reinforce one another? | Trace dependencies, operating changes, staffing, incentives, and capacity. Examine both enabling actions and activities that must stop. | Local incentives reward behavior that defeats the strategic outcome. |
| What would change the choice? | Assumption tests, sensitivity analysis, downside scenarios, and trigger-based review. | Arbitrary percentage shocks replace a causal account of how the business could change. |

A business-model analysis links the user, buyer, payer, delivered value, revenue mechanism, direct costs, support/servicing load, and retained value. Reconcile units and time periods. Test which side of a marketplace or ecosystem creates value and which captures it. Do not assume growth, usage, revenue, or margin is the single correct objective.

For differentiation, compare the factors customers actually choose on. Consider removing, reducing, improving, or creating factors, then check willingness to adopt and delivery cost. A different-looking value curve is not evidence of demand. Validate new demand with the relevant customer population and realistic alternatives.

Use industry structure analysis where suppliers, buyers, substitutes, entry, or rivalry materially change bargaining power or returns. Use firm and segment evidence rather than labeling every force high/medium/low from generic statements.

Stress a quantitative assumption using mechanisms and plausible ranges. Show affected outcomes and correlated assumptions, not just independent one-variable changes. For qualitative assumptions, define observable disconfirming evidence and the likely time to notice. Do not invent a base rate where comparable evidence is missing.

For a review, separate three possible interventions: improve execution of the current choice, change the resource plan, or revise strategy. Explain why the evidence calls for the recommended level of change.

## Check the peer comparison from the bottom up

[Emilie Choi on The Peel](https://www.thespl.it/p/the-coo-playbook-with-emilie-choi) recalls pressure to explain why a peer appeared able to operate with far fewer employees. Rebuild the staffing need from customer count, service obligations and controls before adopting a peer ratio.

List the activities that each business performs, outsources or omits. Where a material activity cannot be reconciled, show the gap explicitly. A lower published headcount can motivate investigation without becoming a staffing target.
