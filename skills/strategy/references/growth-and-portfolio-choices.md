# Growth direction and portfolio choices

Use this reference to choose a growth path, not to classify ideas after the decision. It expands the [framework selector](../../../frameworks/README.md). All example inputs below are synthetic. Named methods come from their cited authors. The application records and calculations are this library's synthesis.

## Define the core before an adjacency

Chris Zook and James Allen's adjacency work starts from the actual core and repeatable strengths. Identify the customers, customer problem, offer, economics, delivery activities, channels, and capabilities responsible for current results. A legal entity, broad industry label, installed account count, or management aspiration is not a sufficiently precise core. Distinguish a profitable repeatable core from an installed base subsidized by bespoke work.

Generate alternatives around six directions: geography, channel, product/service, customer segment, value chain, and new businesses based on core capabilities. This is an options map, not six required initiatives. Analyze growth within the core alongside adjacencies. A new benefit for existing customers can be a product adjacency, but its delivery economics and buyer need still require evidence.

For each option, record the following:

| Field | Required analysis |
|---|---|
| Changed dimensions | Customer, problem, offer, channel, geography, activity system, capabilities, business model, and material rules that change |
| Reused advantage | Specific resource or activity transferred, evidence of customer value, copying constraint, and cost of transfer |
| Required learning | Demand, willingness to pay, route to buyer, service capacity, and competitive response still unknown |
| Effect on core | Shared capacity used, customer/channel conflict, cannibalization, service risk, and possible reinforcement |
| Sequence | Smallest move that tests the proposed repeatable formula and what it enables next |
| Economics and boundary | Incremental contribution/cash, scarce resource, stop loss, and evidence needed before expansion |

Count changes to expose assumptions, not to calculate a probability of success. Two product changes can be harder than five small operational changes. Adjacent distribution does not establish adjacent demand. A strong core is a hypothesis to test, and the method cannot prohibit reinvention when the core is deteriorating. In that case compare renewal or replacement explicitly rather than describing it as a routine adjacency.

Source: [Alistair Corbett and Chris Zook, Hard-core growth](https://www.bain.com/insights/hard-core-growth-bain-research-shows-how-smart-companies-profit-from-their-core-business-beyond). The article describes six directions, overlap with the core, customer economics, and repeatable expansion. This reference does not import its historical success rates as current planning assumptions.

## Turn options into competing strategies

Use Playing to Win to specify winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, required capabilities, and management systems. Write at least the strongest credible alternative, not a straw version of it. Then work backward: what must be true about buyers, alternatives, economics, capabilities, competitor responses, and organizational willingness for this strategy to win?

Make the least-supported consequential condition a test. A condition can be important but already well supported, or uncertain but irrelevant to the choice. Prioritize the uncertainty that can reverse the investment, using a relevant threshold or qualitative observable. Let the strongest skeptic specify fair disconfirming evidence before the test. A workshop vote or completed cascade does not validate the condition.

| Strategy hypothesis | Condition required | Evidence available | Evidence that reverses the choice | Next test and commitment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stated customer/advantage choice | Specific behavior, unit economics or capability | Source, date, population, and limitation | Observable contrary result or calculated threshold | Owner, cost, window, accepted resource and next decision |

Source: [A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin's strategy choice cascade](https://rogerlmartin.com/thought-pillars/strategy). The five choices organize a strategy. They do not by themselves select a financial investment or prove an advantage.

## Use BCG for the appropriate portfolio question

The BCG growth-share matrix examines market growth and relative market share across businesses. State the market boundary and period first. Calculate relative share against the largest rival under the same boundary. A market leader can have relative share above one. A small internal product with growing revenue is not necessarily in a high-growth market.

Treat the four categories as hypotheses about investment and cash generation. Bring actual contribution, cash needs, returns, customer retention, shared capabilities, switching costs, and strategic dependencies to the allocation. Set meaningful cutoffs for this portfolio instead of importing a universal market-growth threshold. Do not add rates, shares, or category labels into a numerical ranking.

When share cannot be measured, report it missing. Do not replace it with feature count, installed users, search interest, or management confidence. In an early market without stable boundaries, explicit scenario and staged-learning analysis can be more useful. A business-unit operator can recommend a portfolio change within the sponsor's scope without owning group capital allocation.

Source: [BCG, Growth Share Matrix](https://www.bcg.com/about/overview/our-history/growth-share-matrix). The source describes the historical framework. Its cash-allocation prescriptions are hypotheses to challenge against current economics and interdependencies.

## Worked choice: serve a new customer segment or add a new service

A synthetic reporting business has a repeatable core in regional distributors. Its standard connectors, direct buying relationship, and onboarding routine have demonstrated value in that segment. A new quarter has a $300,000 investment ceiling and 12 scarce implementation weeks. Two feasible alternatives compete with maintaining the core. All economic figures are assumed incremental 24-month undiscounted contribution, not company valuation.

| Option | Adjacency and reused strength | Unknown that can change the choice | Cost and capacity | Contribution before investment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enter a neighboring US segment | Customer adjacency. Reuse most connectors and route to buyer | Does the new segment accept the standard onboarding promise? | $180,000 and 10 weeks | 90 retained customer-equivalents × $4,000 = $360,000 |
| Add planning assistance for current buyers | Product/service adjacency. Reuse trust and workflow data | Can the service deliver without recurring analyst labor erasing margin? | $120,000 and 6 weeks | 80 retained customer-equivalents × $3,000 = $240,000 |
| Maintain current scope | No adjacency. Preserve scarce capacity for core repair | Is there an urgent core reliability issue that displaces growth? | No new growth investment | No claimed incremental growth contribution |

The options yield $180,000 and $120,000 net respectively. Both together require 16 weeks, so the plan cannot fund them simultaneously even though the costs total $300,000. Choose neighboring-segment expansion under the supplied estimates and absence of a core service breach. Keep planning assistance as the rejected alternative rather than adding it to the roadmap without resources.

Pursue repeatable distributor reporting in the adjacent segment using supported systems. Compete on reliable standard setup. Reuse connectors, narrow onboarding and have the existing acceptance owner reject bespoke exceptions. This is a strategy hypothesis, not a verified new-market result.

The strongest case for planning assistance is access to established buyers with lower customer acquisition uncertainty and lower implementation effort. It wins on net contribution if neighboring-segment retained customer-equivalents fall below 75: 75 × $4,000 - $180,000 = $120,000. The expansion's own break-even is 45. Test buyer acceptance and delivery with a bounded pilot before releasing the full investment. Do not invent a statistically validated sample from these illustrative thresholds. If a material core repair consumes more than two of the twelve weeks, the ten-week expansion no longer fits and the resource choice must reopen.

A repeatable expansion would preserve the same advantage in later segments. If each segment needs a different sales channel, data model, service team, and promise, the observed moves are not yet a repeatable formula.

## Worked BCG check that rejects a mechanical allocation

Consider a separate synthetic two-business portfolio. Storage has 30% share against a largest rival's 20%, market growth 3%, and $10 million annual free contribution after its defined ongoing service costs. A new AI service has 5% share against a rival's 25%, market growth 25%, and needs $6 million annual investment. These share and growth inputs are stipulated, not public market estimates.

Relative shares are 1.5 and 0.2. For this illustrative map, the owner sets high growth at 10% or above and high relative share at 1 or above. These are local example cutoffs, not prescribed universal boundaries. Under those cutoffs, the first is a cash-generation hypothesis and the second a question-mark investment hypothesis. The team must still preserve $5 million of storage resilience and capacity work not already included in its free-contribution figure. Only $5 million remains, so the $6 million proposal is unaffordable without a real resource change. A matrix label cannot create the missing $1 million.

Do not relabel an AI feature within the same subscription as a separate business with invented market share. Shared pricing, storage costs, household membership and retention can make the relevant unit a bundled offer. Reconcile shared economics before applying portfolio labels or claiming cannibalization benefits.

## Deliver a choice, not a framework pack

Provide the actual core, alternatives, reused advantage and capability gaps, coherent choice cascade, decision-changing tests, economics/capacity reconciliation, rejected work, and review condition. Add the BCG map only if the portfolio and share information support it. Use [capability sourcing](capability-sourcing.md) to decide how to obtain what the chosen strategy lacks. Choosing where to grow and choosing build, buy, or partner are separate decisions.

## Test the buyer before funding the adjacency

[Joel Gascoigne](https://buffer.com/resources/vp-product/) describes how Buffer Reply grew from $4,000 to $70,000 in monthly recurring revenue. Its strongest buyers needed enterprise service capabilities outside Buffer's core experience. Revenue growth gave the team a reason to keep investing even as customer fit weakened.

Separate the buyers who share the existing product and channel from buyers who need a new sales and service organization. Compare contribution after supporting each group. If the second group drives the upside, ask for a separately funded business case.

[Microsoft](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2015/07/08/microsoft-announces-restructuring-of-phone-hardware-business) changed its phone hardware objective from standalone growth to supporting its broader ecosystem. When leadership changes the objective, retire the old volume target. Name the benefit a continuing investment must produce elsewhere and who can verify it.
